# THE COMPLETE WES KAO GUIDE TO WEBSITE COPY & COMMUNICATION

_Applying executive communication principles to every page of your website_

---

## PART I: THE FOUNDATIONAL MINDSET

### Take Ownership of the Reaction You're Getting

This is Wes's core philosophy, and it's the starting point for all website copy:

"I often see operators who explain things poorly and then are shocked and horrified when people are confused, or there's skepticism, there's apathy, there are a lot of avoidable questions. And I'm a big proponent of asking myself, 'If I'm not getting the reaction that I'm looking for, how might I be contributing to that?' So instead of blaming other people for not understanding me, I think about: How could I explain this more clearly? How can I be more compelling? How can I anticipate any questions that they might have?"

**Website Application:** If visitors are bouncing, not converting, or asking obvious questions in sales calls that should have been answered on your site—that's a communication problem on your end. Not a "dumb visitor" problem. You can't control who comes to your site, but you can control how clearly you communicate.

### Communication Is a Means to an End

"I see communication as more of a means to an end... where the end is getting the ideal outcome you're looking for. Whether that is buy-in, or making a good decision as a team, or moving to the next step—communication is really in service of that end goal."

**Website Application:** Your copy isn't about self-expression or showing off clever writing. Every sentence should serve the goal of moving visitors to the next step—whether that's signing up, booking a demo, clicking through to learn more, or understanding your value prop deeply enough to tell their colleagues about you.

### The Compounding Value of "Minor" Improvements

"All these little things compound and make a big difference. I often hear people think, 'Well, this individual instance, this individual email, this Slack message is not worth spending a couple more minutes on.' The problem with that line of thinking is that no one instance of something is ever going to feel important enough to spend a little bit more time on. But when you zoom out, that's all your work then."

And: "People claim the point of diminishing returns way too early... It's more likely that your skill level, your creativity, your execution ability was not good enough. And that's fine. Let's admit that to ourselves, because if we admit that, then we can do the hard work of getting better."

**Website Application:** That product page you haven't touched in six months? That help article that's "good enough"? That blog post intro that feels a little slow? These all compound. Your website IS your communication. Every page that's "fine but not great" is hurting you.

## PART II: THE CORE FRAMEWORKS

### Framework 1: The Super-Specific How

This is probably Wes's most famous framework, and it's pure gold for website copy:

"The idea of the super-specific how is that most writers spend too much time on the what and the why, and not enough time on the how. Unless what you're saying is truly controversial, groundbreaking, or new to your audience, you don't need to spend too much time elaborating on the concept itself and why it matters. People really want to know: How do I do this? How do I apply this to my own life? How do I think about the nuances when I'm applying this? What are examples that I can look at that help me better internalize how this really works?"

She gives a concrete example: "If you're writing about product management and communication, you don't want to spend too much time talking about how communication is important for product managers. Most product managers already know that—it's pretty basic. Instead, you want to spend that time talking about how to get buy-in when you don't have positional authority as a product manager."

**Website Application by Page Type:**

|Page Type|What to Cut|What to Add Instead|
|---|---|---|
|**Homepage**|"In today's fast-paced world, teams need better collaboration..."|The specific mechanism of how you help|
|**Product Pages**|Long explanations of why this category matters|Concrete examples of the feature in action|
|**Use Case Pages**|"Marketing teams face many challenges..."|Specific workflows, step-by-step processes|
|**Blog Posts**|Preamble about why the topic is important|Jump to tactical advice, examples, templates|
|**Help Articles**|Background on why users might need this feature|Immediate instructions with screenshots|

**The Test:** Read your page. How long before you get to specific, actionable information? That's your "super-specific how" latency. Lower it.

---

### Framework 2: Start Right Before You Get Eaten by the Bear

"Most people need less context-setting and preamble than you might think. I have a framework that I call 'start right before you get eaten by the bear.' The idea is that if you're telling a story about camping, don't start talking about going to REI to buy a Patagonia jacket, and then booking the campsite, and the website had difficulties, and on the drive over we stopped by the gas station. No one cares about all that. Start right before your friend left a Clif Bar out in their tent and you all almost got mauled by a bear. Get to the juicy part. Start with a little bit of context right before we get to the juicy part. That's the idea—cut out all that backstory scope creep."

**Website Application by Page Type:**

|Page Type|The "Bear" Moment|Cut Everything Before It|
|---|---|---|
|**Homepage**|The transformation your product enables|Company history, industry context, vision statements|
|**Product Pages**|What this feature actually does|Why features like this exist|
|**Case Studies**|The measurable result|How you met the client, lengthy company descriptions|
|**Blog Posts**|The first piece of tactical advice|Lengthy introductions about "why this matters"|
|**About Page**|What makes you different|Founding story preamble (unless that IS your differentiator)|
|**Pricing Page**|The plans and what's included|Paragraphs about your pricing philosophy|

**The Exercise:** Open any page on your site. Scroll down until it gets interesting. Can you start there?

### Framework 3: Sales Before Logistics

This is huge for landing pages and product pages:

"A common mistake I see is overestimating the amount of buy-in that you have from your audience. That looks like jumping straight into talking about the logistics—the details of how to do something, the process—when in reality, your audience has not yet decided if they even want to do the thing."

"What I see operators do in response is go even deeper into the logistics and the how, thinking, 'Oh, if I just explain this more, then that person will want to do it.' When really, a sales note is different than a logistics note. A sales note is meant to get people excited to do the thing you want them to do, and to agree to do it. Only then, after they have bought in, does it make sense to share the logistics."

But importantly—you can do this quickly:

"I actually think that you should always do a little bit of selling, even for situations where people have generally bought in. Because most of us have a lot going on and we're not actively thinking about whatever you're talking about. Even though I agreed to something two weeks ago, by the time you're telling me about it again, I've thought about a billion other things since then."

"You can frame a conversation and sell a bit at the beginning very concisely. I'm not talking about spending 15 minutes out of a 30-minute meeting selling. I'm talking about one to two minutes, even a couple sentences, and then transitioning into the main thing you want to talk about."

**Website Application:**

**Homepage Structure:**

1. **Emotional hook** (1-2 sentences of "sales") — Why this matters to them
2. **Brief social proof** — Who already trusts you
3. **The "how"** (logistics) — Now explain how it works
4. **Features** — Specific capabilities
5. **CTA** — Tie back to the promise

**Product Page Structure:**

1. **The payoff** (sales) — What this feature enables
2. **How it works** (logistics) — The mechanism
3. **Details** — Configuration, specifications
4. **CTA** — Get started

**Common Mistake:** Feature pages that open with technical details before establishing why anyone should care.

---

### Framework 4: Bottom Line Up Front (with Context Available)

"I'll usually put the most important point at the top—the TL;DR, if you will, the gist—and then I'll say 'Context:' and then there might be multiple paragraphs of context below for anyone who wants additional thinking on how I got to this decision or how I thought about this. But if they already agree with the decision and kind of know that context, they don't need to keep reading."

This connects to the MINTO pyramid concept: "In business, you often want to start with 'Here's my conclusion' and then 'Here's why'—versus 'Here's all the things I've done, here's all my thinking, here's all my data points, and then now here's my conclusion' at the end."

And the biggest sin: "The worst—which happens a lot—is mixing all of those things with the action item or decision. So the action items and decisions are kind of interspersed randomly throughout a bunch of context, thought process, factors that you looked at, downsides. It's all just interwoven, and so your reader doesn't know which parts are FYI or background versus what is the thing that you want their response on."

**Website Application by Page Type:**

|Page Type|Bottom Line Up Front|Context Below|
|---|---|---|
|**Homepage**|Your value prop in the hero|How you deliver, who you serve|
|**Pricing Page**|Plans and prices visible immediately|FAQs, comparisons, detailed breakdowns below|
|**Help Articles**|The answer/instruction first|Why this works, edge cases, background later|
|**Blog Posts**|Key takeaway in first paragraph|Evidence, examples, nuance below|
|**Case Studies**|The headline metric/result|The story of how you got there|
|**Use Case Pages**|"This is who we help and how"|Detailed workflows and examples|

**The Test:** Can a visitor get the main point in 5 seconds? Is additional context available for those who want it?

### Framework 5: MOO (Most Obvious Objection)

This might be the most actionable framework for conversion-focused copy:

"MOO stands for Most Obvious Objection. A lot of times we're surprised by the questions that we get, especially in meetings where we feel blindsided—that was unexpected—and then we're on our back foot. When really, if you thought for even two minutes about what are obvious objections that I'm likely to get when I share this, you often immediately come up with what some of those things are. Are you going to be able to anticipate every single objection? No. But can you anticipate the obvious ones? Absolutely."

"Whatever I'm about to say, I think: How might someone disagree with this? What might an objection be? It's a really good mental filter because it encourages you to think a couple steps ahead. If I'm about to say this, the person may then say this to me. Well, if I take that into account, I can volunteer that information upfront or frame it in a way where they're less likely to think that's an issue."

And there's a secondary benefit: "Thinking ahead to what might be the most obvious objection actually prompts you to realize that maybe there was a gap in what you were planning to present. And then you now have an opportunity to strengthen that pitch before you say it out loud."

**Website Application by Page Type:**

|Page Type|Common MOOs to Address|How to Address|
|---|---|---|
|**Homepage**|"Is this different from [competitor]?"|Positioning statement, differentiation section|
|**Pricing Page**|"Is this worth the price?" "Are there hidden fees?"|Value framing, transparent pricing, money-back guarantee|
|**Product Pages**|"Is this hard to implement?" "Will this work for my use case?"|Integration info, use case examples, time-to-value|
|**Case Studies**|"That company is nothing like mine"|Include variety of industries/sizes, or be specific about who this applies to|
|**Checkout/Signup**|"What happens after I sign up?" "Can I cancel?"|Next steps preview, cancellation policy visible|
|**Help Articles**|"What if this doesn't work?"|Troubleshooting sections, contact support CTA|

**The Exercise:** For every page, spend 2 minutes listing objections a skeptical visitor might have. Then check: are any addressed?

## PART III: CLARITY & CONCISION

### Concise ≠ Brief

This is crucial and often misunderstood:

"One of my pet peeves is when people are too concise and they equate being concise with being brief. Being concise is not about absolute word count; it's about economy of words. It's about the density of the insight that you're sharing. You can have a 300-word memo that's meandering and long-winded, and a thousand-word memo that is tight and concise."

And here's why people struggle:

"A lot of advice about being concise misses an important point. We've all heard 'Don't bury the lead,' 'Cut to the chase,' 'Main point at the top,' 'Bottom line up front,' right? All of these pithy aphorisms assume that you actually know what your core point is. You can't cut to the chase unless you know what the chase is. You can't unbury the lead unless you know what the lead is. That, I found, is the bottleneck to being concise. It's actually not being clear about what you are thinking—that's what's leading to being long-winded."

**Website Application:**

- A long page isn't necessarily bad. A meandering page is.
- Before editing for length, make sure you know your core message
- If your page feels long-winded, you may not have crystallized your point yet
- Short pages that don't communicate clearly are worse than longer pages that do

**The Test:** Can you state the single main point of this page in one sentence? If not, that's your problem—not the word count.

---

### The Blast Radius of Bad Copy

"I think the blast radius of a poorly written memo is way bigger than most people think. If you're just shooting off a message in a Slack channel with 15 other people and it's confusing—you didn't include information you should have included—there's going to be a bunch of back and forth. All 15 of these people are reading this being like, 'Okay, what do I do with this?' Whereas if you had just taken another look at it, those 15 people would be off to the races."

**Website Application:** Think about the compounding cost of unclear website copy:

- Visitors who don't understand and bounce
- Sales calls where prospects ask questions already answered on your site
- Word-of-mouth that never happens because visitors can't explain what you do
- Support tickets that could have been prevented by clearer help docs
- Prospects who misunderstand your positioning and think you're not for them

Every unclear page creates ripples.

---

### Signposting: Power Words for Clarity

"Signposting is using certain words, phrases, formatting, and an overall structure in your writing that helps guide your reader and signals what is coming in the rest of the post. This is especially helpful if you have a long memo. It adds structure to where we're going and what certain sections or paragraphs are about."

Wes's favorite signposting words:

- **"For example"** — signals you're about to illustrate
- **"Because"** — signals you're about to explain reasoning
- **"As a next step"** — eyes automatically zoom to this
- **"First, second, third"** — creates clear structure
- **"The most important part is..."** — directs attention
- **"The part that surprised us was..."** — creates intrigue

"You're signaling that whatever comes after this thing is something you may want to pay attention to. It's a great way not only to add structure, but to also grab people's attention back if it has strayed."

**Website Application by Page Type:**

|Page Type|Useful Signposting|
|---|---|
|**Product Pages**|"Here's how it works:" → "For example:" → "The result:"|
|**Use Case Pages**|"First, you..." → "Then..." → "Finally..."|
|**Help Articles**|"Step 1:" → "Note:" → "If you're stuck:"|
|**Blog Posts**|"The key insight:" → "For example:" → "Here's what to do:"|
|**Comparison Pages**|"The biggest difference:" → "Where we're similar:" → "Bottom line:"|
|**Case Studies**|"The challenge:" → "What we did:" → "The result:"|

---

### Formatting: Less Is More (But Intentional)

"I really hate excessive formatting. I've seen memos where 30% of the note was bolded. That just negates the entire point of bolding, because if everything is bolded, then nothing is being highlighted. Using formatting in general more sparingly than you think you have to is probably a good rule of thumb."

On bullets:

"I dislike when people overuse bullets and sentence fragments—phrases in bullets when they should use complete sentences that actually show the connected tissue between ideas, that show the logical flow of what it is that you're saying. It feels faster and more concise to put bullets and fragments, but a lot of times your reader on the other end of that is needing to decipher and interpret and guess what you actually meant."

Here's her litmus test:

"If you had to turn your sentence fragment into a full sentence, a lot of times it's actually harder than you think. Because you realize that you actually didn't really know exactly what you meant. That's a great litmus test of 'Was that idea fully thought out?' Because if it was, you should be able to really quickly turn it into a complete sentence. And many times, you actually can't."

**Website Application:**

- Bold the ONE thing you want people to remember per section—not everything
- Use bullets for actual lists, not as a substitute for coherent prose
- Test: Can you turn each bullet into a complete sentence easily? If not, you may not know what you're saying
- Don't let formatting substitute for clear thinking

**Common Website Offenders:**

- Feature grids where every cell is bolded
- Bullet lists that are really incomplete thoughts
- Headers every 50 words (breaking up the thinking, not enhancing it)
- Bold + italic + underline + different color (pick one)

## PART IV: BUILDING TRUST & CREDIBILITY

### Speaking with the Right Level of Confidence

"I find that people tend to naturally be on the spectrum a little bit too confident as a baseline, or not confident enough."

On overconfidence:

"People who are too confident might state hypotheses as if they are fact. That really bothers me. If you say 'This is X' or 'This will X,' that is different than saying 'This could X' or 'This might X' or 'This will increase the likelihood of X.' I'm a big proponent of speaking accurately. You can avoid a lot of problems if you speak accurately about your level of conviction and about the actual amount of evidence that you have for something."

On under-confidence:

"I have some clients where their CEO asked them to share some recommendations with another team because they've run something before. They share all this amazing information, and at the end they're like, 'Oh, but you can ignore everything I just said. Obviously, make your own decision. Do what you think is best. And if you want to just ignore everything, that's totally cool too.' You just didn't have to say that."

**Website Application:**

|Situation|Overconfident (Avoid)|Under-confident (Avoid)|Right Level|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Feature claim**|"This will transform your workflow"|"This might help you somewhat"|"Designed to reduce workflow time by up to 40%"|
|**Case study**|"Customers always see 10x ROI"|"Some customers have maybe seen improvements"|"Acme Corp saw 47% improvement in 6 months"|
|**Comparison**|"We're better than everyone"|"We're probably okay for some people"|"Best for teams that need X; less ideal for Y"|
|**Support**|"This definitely fixes the problem"|"Try this if you want, I guess"|"This resolves the issue in most cases"|

**The Rule:** Bold claims require bold proof. Modest claims need less support. Match your evidence to your claims.

---

### Don't Be a Single-Minded Martyr

"A single-minded martyr is someone who very much has an agenda, who wants the recommendation to go through, and is presenting a bunch of evidence supporting that direction."

Related, on building trust:

"When you do a pros and cons list but they're skewed and you're leaving some things out of the cons list, it makes the reader suspicious and they can't trust you anymore. Whereas if you do a pros and cons list but at the top you say, 'My recommendation is X. Here are pros and cons of that,' or 'Here are some risks associated with it'—you're building trust with your reader because you were direct in saying, 'Here's my recommendation. Here's what I'm advocating for. And also, here are some downsides to that.'"

**Website Application:**

- **Comparison pages** that only show where you win destroy trust
- Being honest about **who you're NOT right for** builds credibility
- "Best for X, less ideal for Y" is more believable than "Best for everyone"
- Acknowledge **tradeoffs**—it shows you're not just selling

**Examples:**

- **Bad:** "Our product is perfect for everyone from startups to enterprises"
- **Good:** "Built for fast-moving startups (enterprises needing SOC 2 should check out our Enterprise plan)"
- **Bad:** Comparison page where you have checkmarks in every row and competitors have X's
- **Good:** Honest comparison acknowledging where competitors excel, while showing your differentiation

---

### Avoid Accidental Points of View

"A lot of times when we write a sentence, there's actually already a point of view baked in, but you don't want it to be an accidental point of view."

Wes gives an example of reviewing a team member's writing: "The way that she had phrased it, the obvious conclusion was, 'Oh, well, we should just stick with our current one.' So I asked her, 'Is that your recommendation? Because if it is, then great—because you're kind of leading the reader to that conclusion. But if it's not, you're asking a leading question that is skewing the results.'"

**Website Application:**

- Review your copy for unintentional framing
- Are you accidentally positioning yourself as enterprise-only when you want SMBs?
- Does your tone accidentally exclude certain audiences?
- Is your comparison page accidentally making the competitor look good?
- Does your pricing page accidentally make the cheap plan look like the right choice when you want people on the premium?

---

### Have a "Spiky Point of View"

Wes mentions this concept: "I guess my spiky point of view is that more people should learn the craft of writing and the technical aspects of writing—not just look at what other people are doing to try to get audience engagement."

**Website Application:**

- What's your contrarian take on your industry?
- What do you believe that competitors don't?
- That POV should come through in your messaging
- Vanilla messaging blends in; spiky messaging stands out
- Your "About" page and homepage hero are great places for POV

**Example:**

- **Vanilla:** "We help teams collaborate better"
- **Spiky:** "Most collaboration tools add noise. We believe fewer features means faster work."

## PART V: CONTENT STRATEGY (BLOGS, HELP DOCS)

### The Content Hierarchy of BS

This framework is particularly relevant for blog content and thought leadership:

"If you imagine a pyramid or triangle: at the bottom, there's more room for BS. At the very top of the triangle, there's less room for BS. What's at the bottom of that triangle? Twitter, podcasts, short articles. It's basically situations that are one-directional, where people can't really challenge what you're saying... You just say it, you leave it there, and then you get to walk away without needing to defend it."

"But as you move up the triangle, the content hierarchy of BS, there's less and less room for BS. Long-form, in-depth articles: less room for BS. You have to defend the idea; you have to convince your reader."

**Website Application:**

|Content Type|BS Tolerance|Implication|
|---|---|---|
|**Social posts/headlines**|Higher|Need to grab attention, but don't over-promise|
|**Short blog posts**|Medium-High|Can make bold claims but should substantiate|
|**In-depth guides**|Medium|Must defend your ideas, show your work|
|**Help documentation**|Low|Must be accurate, testable, verifiable|
|**Product pages**|Low|Claims should match actual product capability|
|**Case studies**|Very Low|Real numbers, real customers, verifiable|

**The Implication for Blogs:** "There are books that could be ten-page blog posts, and there are books where every page earns its real estate. So there's still a little bit of nuance in the hierarchy, but in general, as you move up that hierarchy, there's less and less room for BS."

**For your blog:** Ask yourself—does every paragraph earn its space? Could this whole post be a tweet? If so, make it a tweet. If your post has genuine depth, let it breathe.

---

### Help Documentation: Zero Room for BS

Help pages sit near the top of the hierarchy—there's almost no room for BS. Visitors coming to help articles are frustrated, searching for answers, and have low patience for preamble.

**Apply Wes's Frameworks to Help Docs:**

1. **Super-Specific How:** Jump immediately to the instructions
2. **Start Before the Bear:** The "bear" is the solution—don't make them read context
3. **Bottom Line Up Front:** Answer first, explain later
4. **Signposting:** "Step 1:", "Note:", "If this doesn't work:"

**Help Article Structure:**

```
[Title: Exactly what this article solves]

[2-line summary of the solution]

## How to [do the thing]
1. Step one
2. Step two
3. Step three

## Troubleshooting
- If X happens, do Y
- If that doesn't work, contact support

## Background (optional)
Why this feature works this way (for those who want to understand)
```

---

### Blog Posts: Balance Depth and Accessibility

**Apply Wes's Frameworks:**

1. **Super-Specific How:** Cut the "why this matters" intro—get to tactics
2. **Start Before the Bear:** Open with the insight, not the setup
3. **Signposting:** Guide readers through longer posts
4. **MOO:** Address objections to your advice ("But what if...")

**Blog Post Opening Test:**

**Weak opening (too much preamble):**

> "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, marketers face unprecedented challenges. With the rise of AI, automation, and changing consumer preferences, it's more important than ever to stay ahead of the curve. In this post, we'll explore some tactics that might help you navigate these complex waters."

**Strong opening (starts at the bear):**

> "Most marketing teams waste 40% of their time on tasks that could be automated. Here are the three automations that saved our team 15 hours per week—with setup instructions you can implement today."

## PART VI: PRACTICAL APPLICATION BY PAGE TYPE

### Homepage

**Primary Goal:** Get visitors to understand what you do and why they should care, fast.

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Super-Specific How**|Hero copy should explain the mechanism, not just the category|
|**Start Before the Bear**|Lead with transformation, not industry context|
|**Sales Before Logistics**|Emotional hook first, then "how it works"|
|**Bottom Line Up Front**|Value prop visible in 5 seconds|
|**MOO**|Address "Is this for me?" and "Why should I trust you?"|
|**Signposting**|Clear sections: "How it works," "Who it's for," "Results"|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Can a visitor explain what you do after 5 seconds?
- [ ] Is there emotional resonance before feature explanation?
- [ ] Are the most common objections addressed (or social proof provided)?
- [ ] Is the CTA clear and the next step obvious?

---

### Product/Feature Pages

**Primary Goal:** Convince visitors this specific feature/product solves their problem.

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Super-Specific How**|Show the feature in action, not just describe it|
|**Sales Before Logistics**|Lead with the payoff (what it enables), then how it works|
|**MOO**|Address "Is this hard to set up?" and "Does this work for my use case?"|
|**Right Level of Confidence**|Specific claims with evidence, not vague superlatives|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Does the page open with the benefit, not the feature?
- [ ] Are there specific examples, screenshots, or demos?
- [ ] Is implementation/difficulty addressed?
- [ ] Are edge cases or limitations acknowledged honestly?

---

### Use Case Pages

**Primary Goal:** Help visitors self-identify and see themselves using your product.

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Super-Specific How**|Walk through the actual workflow for this use case|
|**Signposting**|"First, you do X. Then, you see Y. The result is Z."|
|**MOO**|Address use-case-specific concerns ("What if I have legacy data?")|
|**Avoid Accidental POV**|Make sure you're speaking to the right audience for this page|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Would someone in this role see themselves on this page?
- [ ] Is the workflow specific to their situation, not generic?
- [ ] Are relevant integrations/contexts addressed?
- [ ] Does the page feel like it was written FOR them?

---

### Persona Pages

**Primary Goal:** Make a specific type of buyer feel "this is for me."

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Sales Before Logistics**|Lead with their pain, not your solution|
|**Spiky POV**|Show you understand their world view|
|**MOO**|Address persona-specific objections ("I'm not technical")|
|**Right Level of Confidence**|Match the language/formality of the persona|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Does the copy use language this persona actually uses?
- [ ] Are the pain points specific to their role, not generic?
- [ ] Is the proof relevant to their context (case studies, testimonials from similar roles)?
- [ ] Do they see people like them using this?

---

### Pricing Pages

**Primary Goal:** Convert consideration to action with minimal friction.

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Bottom Line Up Front**|Plans and prices visible immediately|
|**MOO**|Address "Is this worth it?" "What's hidden?" "Can I change later?"|
|**Sales Before Logistics**|Brief value reminder before diving into plan details|
|**Building Trust**|Transparent pricing, no surprises, clear terms|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Can visitors see pricing within 2 seconds of landing?
- [ ] Is there a brief value reminder (not just plan grids)?
- [ ] Are common objections addressed (hidden fees, cancellation, refunds)?
- [ ] Is the recommended plan clear without being pushy?

---

### Help/Support Pages

**Primary Goal:** Solve the visitor's problem as fast as possible.

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Super-Specific How**|Instructions immediately, no preamble|
|**Start Before the Bear**|Answer first, context later|
|**Signposting**|"Step 1:", "Note:", "If this doesn't work:"|
|**Bottom Line Up Front**|TL;DR of the solution at the top|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Is the answer visible within the first screen?
- [ ] Are steps numbered and clear?
- [ ] Is there a "this didn't work" fallback?
- [ ] Is context/background available but not required reading?

---

### Blog Content

**Primary Goal:** Provide value that builds trust and attracts the right audience.

|Framework|Application|
|---|---|
|**Super-Specific How**|Lead with tactics, not "why this matters"|
|**Start Before the Bear**|Open with the insight, not the setup|
|**Content Hierarchy of BS**|Earn every paragraph—depth or brevity, not fluff|
|**Signposting**|Guide readers through longer pieces|
|**Spiky POV**|Have a point of view, not just "10 tips"|

**Checklist:**

- [ ] Does the intro grab or does it preamble?
- [ ] Is there a clear takeaway in the first paragraph?
- [ ] Does every section earn its space?
- [ ] Could a reader skim and still get value (via signposting)?
- [ ] Is there a point of view, or is this generic advice?

## PART VII: THE CRAFT OF GOOD WRITING

### Study the Mechanics

Wes's strong POV: "I think a lot of people learn writing from mimicking other people and kind of learning by analogy, especially on Twitter or on social. I think that can be useful to a certain point, but I also think there's a lot of benefit in studying the craft of writing off of social."

"My spiky point of view is that more people should learn the craft of writing and the technical aspects of writing—not just look at what other people are doing to try to get audience engagement, but actually improve your ability to precisely say what you mean, convey the level of conviction that you have, and not accidentally mislead people with your words because you didn't know that the way you wrote something could potentially mislead them."

**Wes's Book Recommendations:**

- _**On Writing Well**_ by William Zinsser — specifically for cutting unnecessary words
- _**It Was the Best of Sentences, It Was the Worst of Sentences**_ by June Casagrande — mechanics and technical craft
- _**Better Business Writing**_ by Harvard Business Press — business writing fundamentals
- _**Several Short Sentences About Writing**_ — the power of short sentences

---

### Actually Look at What You Wrote

"There's a layer of advice under this that you're not saying that I'm going to say, which is: actually look at the thing you wrote at least once before you share it. Even doing that, you can often spot a lot of low-hanging fruit."

**Website Application:** Read your pages out loud. You'll catch:

- Sentences that don't make sense
- Tone mismatches
- Unnecessary words
- Missing transitions
- Confusing structure

---

### The Investment Mindset

"I like thinking about it as a little bit more upfront investment. And it is an investment. It's not just time. It's an investment because yes, it takes a little bit longer to make a Slack message a little bit better. But net-net, if you save a bunch of questions and back and forth and people asking you things that you don't think they should be asking, then by investing a little bit of upfront effort, you've prevented all that from happening."

**Website Application:** A few more hours on a landing page that converts 2% better is worth thousands over its lifetime. A help article that actually solves the problem saves dozens of support tickets. The investment pays dividends.

## PART VIII: SUMMARY CHECKLISTS

### Before Publishing Any Page

**Structure:**

- [ ] Does it start at the bear moment? (No unnecessary preamble)
- [ ] Sales before logistics? (Emotional buy-in before how-to details)
- [ ] Lead with conclusion? (Main point visible immediately)
- [ ] TL;DR at top, context available below?

**Clarity:**

- [ ] Is it concise, not just brief? (Dense with insight, not just short)
- [ ] Have I used signposting words? (For example, Because, As a next step)
- [ ] Is formatting intentional and minimal? (Not everything bolded)
- [ ] Can all bullet points be complete sentences? (If not, unclear thinking)

**Trust:**

- [ ] Right level of confidence? (Not over-claiming, not hedging)
- [ ] Honest about tradeoffs? (Not pretending you're perfect for everyone)
- [ ] POV comes through? (Not vanilla, has a point of view)
- [ ] No accidental framing? (Saying what you mean to say)

**Objection Handling:**

- [ ] MOO check done? (Identified and addressed obvious objections)
- [ ] Proof placed near claims? (Social proof, case studies where needed)

**Craft:**

- [ ] Actually read it out loud?
- [ ] Every paragraph earns its space?
- [ ] Someone unfamiliar could understand?

### The Four Questions

Before publishing, ask:

1. **MOO:** What's the most obvious objection on this page? Is it addressed?
2. **Super-specific how:** Am I telling people _how_ this helps, or just _that_ it does?
3. **Bear check:** Where does the value start? Can I cut everything before it?
4. **One sentence test:** Can I state the main point of this page in one sentence? If not, I don't know what I'm saying yet.

### Wes's Measuring Stick

"Think about how long does it take me right now to get to the reaction I'm looking for from my recipient. If it takes a bunch of back and forth and a bunch of friction, then that's my baseline."

**For websites, that means:**

- How long until visitors understand what you do?
- How many clicks to the information they need?
- How many support tickets could have been prevented by clearer docs?
- How many sales calls include questions your site should have answered?

Track these. Reduce the friction. That's the goal.
