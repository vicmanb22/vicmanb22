## Theorycrafting

So before we dive into the GPS system, there is one key piece of theory that this entire system is based around.

Now this might sound obvious, but it's surprisingly powerful: **every goal you want to accomplish is merely a series of tasks or actions that you do.** So if you are not accomplishing your goals, you are simply not doing the things that it would take you to accomplish your goals.

If you didn't need to do stuff, then your goal would just happen automatically. And the reason it hasn't happened yet is because you're not doing the right things. That is either:

1. You don't know what the right things are to do, or
2. You do know what the right things to do are, but you're not actually doing them

Pretty much all of goal achievement—all of the evidence around it—can basically be summed down to those things:
- What is the goal?
- What are the actions that are actually going to get you there?
- How do you get yourself to actually do those things?

It's these three concepts that the GPS system is based around.

For example, let's say I wanted to get fit. In order to get fit, I have to do a series of actions like eating healthily, hitting the gym, and doing some cardio. If I wanted to start a business, there would be a series of actions like figuring out my idea, coming up with a product, and finding people to buy it.

The whole idea behind the GPS system is that we are breaking down these three components into an actionable system with a nice acronym that we can use:

1. **Firstly**, to actually achieve the goals that we set
2. **Secondly**, as a diagnostic tool—where if we are struggling with something, we can just run it through the GPS system and then we'll see exactly why we are struggling

## The GPS System

The GPS system stands for **Goal, Plan, and System**. Within each of these components, there are three factors or three things that we want to be considering.

This might sound like a lot, but literally it takes less than 10 minutes per goal that you set—maybe even 5 minutes—to figure out the three components of the GPS system. And if you just follow the system, you are drastically more likely to actually achieve the goals that you set.

## G: Goal

When it comes to setting the goal in the first place, there are two broad mistakes people make.

### Mistake #1: Not Being Specific Enough

The first mistake is not being clear, specific, or concrete enough about what the goal actually is. What is the thing you actually want to do?

Things I hear a lot when I do events is someone will set the goal that they want to "stop procrastinating." Okay, but what does stop procrastinating actually mean? You've probably tried to set the goal of stop procrastinating for a decade and it hasn't succeeded. So there's something going wrong here.

Similarly, I speak to people who say that their goal is to "start a YouTube channel." But right now, you could go on youtube.com, click "new channel," and congratulations—you've started a YouTube channel. Have you accomplished the goal? Well, probably not, because when you say "start a YouTube channel," you actually probably mean some things other than that. But you haven't yet taken the time to get specific and concrete about what you actually mean by that particular goal.

**The way we avoid this mistake is by getting really specific on what we are actually trying to do.** The trick here is in the specificity. It is so much easier to work towards a specific and concrete goal compared to a vague or abstract one.

I often hear people say, "I want to start a business" or "I want to start a lifestyle business because I want financial freedom." Okay, that's great, but that's a very vague, abstract goal. "Start a business for financial freedom"—what does financial freedom mean? How much money are we talking?

Starting a business that makes you $100K a year, $500K, or $1 million a year is very different than starting a business that you can sell for $300 million. Those approaches require drastically different paths, and both of them could be a business that gets you to "financial freedom." So what actually is the thing that we are going after?

### Mistake #2: Goals That Aren't Emotionally Compelling

The second big mistake people make when it comes to goals is choosing goals that are not emotionally compelling enough. **You really need to understand why you care about the goal.**

A lot of us choose goals from a sense of "should" or a sense of obligation. "Oh, I really should take care of my health. I really should do that thing because of society, parents, or what people will think."

But in general, it is so much easier to achieve a goal if you have some compelling reasons as to why you care about actually achieving that goal. Ideally, those reasons would be intrinsically motivated, not just extrinsically motivated.

The evidence on this is clear: People who follow goals for extrinsic purposes like fame, money, or status are:
- (a) A lot less likely to actually hit those goals
- (b) Less likely to enjoy the process of hitting those goals

So we want to get clear on **why**. Why do we care about this specific thing?

### The Third Component: Anti-Goals

The third component of the G in GPS is: **What are your anti-goals?** What are the things you want to avoid en route to achieving your goal?

Let's say your goal was to build a $100,000-a-year lifestyle business in the next 12 months. That would be a reasonable goal—it's quite specific. Maybe your reason why is because you really want time freedom. You want the autonomy to be able to control your time, to be able to pick your kids up from school. That's a reasonable "why" behind it.

And you might set the anti-goal: "Along the route to building this business, I don't want to have to work on weekends. I don't want to work more than 40 hours a week." These are all good constraints that you can set on yourself because it's not just about achieving our goals—it's about achieving our goals without torpedoing the other aspects of our life.

### The Three Questions for Goal Setting

1. **What** specifically is the goal?
2. **Why** do you actually care about achieving it? What are your reasons?
3. **What are the anti-goals or constraints?** What are the things you want to avoid en route to achieving the goal?

### Example: Health Goal

For me, one of my health goals is: **I want to reduce my visceral fat levels by 50%.** That is the "what." It's specific. It's tied to a number. In general, whenever you can put a number on your goal, that's a hack for getting more specific, quantifiable, and clear about what you're actually trying to do.

**Why** do I want to decrease my visceral fat levels? Well, I recently became a father, and I would like to not die of a heart attack particularly soon. Visceral fat levels directly contribute to your risk of a heart attack or stroke. I have a family history of heart disease, and thanks to my South Asian genetics, even though I don't look that fat, I actually have quite a lot of visceral fat in my abdomen according to DEXA scans that I've done. I would like to be around for my wife and child and hopefully future children without dropping dead of a myocardial infarction overly soon.

**Anti-goals:** Yes, I want to reduce my visceral fat levels, but I don't want the process to eat into the time I spend with my wife and daughter. So I don't really want to be going on runs in the evenings, for example, because that's family time.

## P: Plan

Having established our goal, the next thing we want to do is figure out what is the plan.

### The Goals vs. Systems Debate

There is a fascinating debate that goes on in the world of personal development around goal setting—specifically about the importance of goals versus systems.

**The Systems Camp:** This was popularized by James Clear in his wonderful book *Atomic Habits*. The general vibe people took away is that you don't need to worry too much about the goals—what matters is the system. The system is sort of like the daily habits. The example he used was that everyone who runs the 100-meter sprint in the Olympics has the goal of winning a gold medal. But obviously not all of them can win. Generally, the person who wins is because they had the best system or the best habits in order to practice and train to get to that particular goal.

This is all well and good. However, if your goal is to win the gold medal in the 100 meters at the Olympics, that is a very different system you would follow compared to winning the gold medal in swimming at the Olympics, or even getting a girlfriend, or starting a business. That's not to say the system is not important, but the goal does in fact define what you're actually doing and what system you follow.

**The Goals Camp:** On the other side, you have people that don't really care about systems or plans—they just care about the goals. This is sort of like the manifestation/law of attraction folks who say, "If you just have crystal clarity about exactly what you want, the universe will conspire to give it to you."

The classic criticism is: Okay, but you can have a goal to become a millionaire, but unless you follow a plan and do stuff—follow some kind of system to get there—it's not actually going to happen.

**Both camps have some kind of merit.** The plan is the bridge between the goal and the system:
- The **goal** is the destination you're trying to get to
- The **system** is putting one foot in front of another to get there
- The **plan** is your roadmap—the path you follow to get to the goal

### The Three Components of the Plan

#### 1. The Three to Five Major Moves

The question you want to ask yourself is: **What are the 3-5 major moves that will get me to my goal?**

For example, if my goal was to reduce my visceral fat by 50%, the 3-5 major moves I might define as:
1. 10,000 steps per day
2. Hitting 150 grams of protein per day
3. Staying underneath 2,200 calories a day
4. Three sessions of Zone 2 cardio per week
5. Three sessions of weight training with progressive overload to avoid losing muscle

Those are five major moves that, if I followed and actually did them, would be pretty likely to get me to my goal of reducing my visceral fat levels. This is the plan—the plan that you follow to get to the goal.

#### 2. Is the Plan Realistic?

The next part of the plan is asking ourselves: **Is the plan actually realistic?**

**In Theory:** Will the plan get us to our goals? Assuming we followed the plan to 100% accuracy, would we actually get to the goal as a result? Does the plan work in theory? Put a percentage on that.

**In Practice:** Will I actually follow the plan? What are the chances of me actually following through on this plan?

This is a really, really important part of the process. A lot of people are very good at setting goals—they know what they want—but the plan they choose to follow just doesn't make sense.

**Example of a Bad Plan:**

I know people who have the goal of getting to $10,000 a month in passive income. The plan they want to follow is:
1. Posting on Instagram once a week
2. Trying to get companies to sponsor them for posting on Instagram once a week
3. Doing this for about 6 months before giving up

Now, even if you followed that plan—posted on Instagram once a week, reached out to brands, and did it for 6 months—would that get you to $10,000 a month in passive income? **Absolutely not.**

There are all sorts of issues with that plan:
- Posting on Instagram once a week is really just not enough to get anywhere
- Brand deals aren't really passive income—you'd probably want to create your own digital product
- Depending on your niche, Instagram might not even be the best platform—LinkedIn, YouTube, or TikTok might be better

Often what I find is that people just don't take the 3-4 minutes to think: What is the plan I actually want to follow? Does the plan even make sense in theory?

**Then, even if the plan makes sense in theory, ask:** Will I actually follow the plan?

For example, if my goal was to reduce visceral fat and part of my plan was running for 5 hours a day—okay, in theory that will massively increase my caloric expenditure, put me in a calorie deficit, and probably reduce my visceral fat. But what are the chances that I'm actually going to run for 5 hours a day? Pretty low.

**Therefore, if any of these numbers is under 80%** (my threshold), that is a sign that I need to rethink my plan.

#### 3. The Crystal Ball Method

The final component is something I call the **Crystal Ball Method**. This is based on research from psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, who came up with the WOOP method.

The whole vibe is: Imagine a crystal ball. Project into the future where you did not achieve your goal or did not follow the plan. **What are the top three reasons why you didn't follow the plan? What are the top three reasons why it didn't work?**

In my visceral fat example:
1. I blow over my calorie limit of 2,200—I eat way more calories, so I'm never going to reduce fat
2. I lose a lot of muscle because I haven't hit my 150g of protein with enough training stimulus to retain muscle
3. I don't do my 10,000 steps a day, which means I end up in a caloric surplus rather than deficit—and the whole plan goes out the window

This is mental forecasting. You're predicting what obstacles you're going to run into before getting started with the goal. **Then you come up with a plan to address those obstacles.**

Using my fat loss example:
- **Obstacle:** How do I make sure I stay under 2,200 calories? **Solution:** Track on MyFitnessPal
- **Obstacle:** How do I make sure I stay at 150g of protein? **Solution:** Track on MyFitnessPal
- **Obstacle:** How do I make sure I get 10,000 steps a day even when I'm busy? **Solution:** I have a walking treadmill, and I can track my step count on my Apple Watch. If I'm in Zoom calls, I walk on the treadmill to passively get those steps in without actually having to go out for a walk

## S: System

Now we get to the boring bit—the system. There are three components of a system.

- The **goal** was: Where do I want to be going? What's my destination?
- The **plan** was: What do I need to do to get there?
- The **system** answers: How will I make sure I stick to the plan? What daily and weekly actions do I need to do to make sure I actually stick to the plan?

Because again, lots of us are very good at setting goals, but we suck at sticking to those goals. There is an execution gap. We have the intention to do the thing, and then we don't do the bloody thing. And the reason is because we have not stuck to the plan—assuming we have a plan in the first place.

### Component #1: Tracking

This meta-analysis collected 138 different studies covering nearly 20,000 participants. They found that **people who monitor their progress towards a goal—who track their progress—are way more likely to actually achieve the goal** compared to people who did not track.

The classic weight loss example: If you just step on a set of scales once a day, you are likely to lose weight because simply being aware of the number makes you start making micro-decisions that cause you to lose weight.

Similarly, ever since I got a WHOOP band, I've been aware of my sleep score. And my sleep has improved really without me having to do anything other than looking at the number every day. When I see things like "my sleep score was only 64%," it nudges me to do sensible things like get an earlier night and make sure the room is set to the right temperature.

**It is enormously helpful to track your progress towards your goal.** The question you want to ask yourself is: For whatever goal I'm following, for whatever plan I'm following, how can I track my progress towards it?

**Examples:**
- When I was writing my book *Feel Good Productivity*, I made the most progress when I had a Google Sheet tracking how many words I was writing every day
- When studying for my exams at Cambridge University Medical School, my highest performances were the ones where I had a Google Sheet tracking the syllabus, color-coding it red/yellow/green depending on how confident I felt about each topic
- People who go to the gym and end up getting jacked tend to track their workouts—they don't just go to the gym and hope for the best
- People training for a marathon are far more likely to actually run a marathon if they're tracking their runs, looking at their heart rate, and adjusting their plan as they go

### Component #2: Reminders

Ask yourself: **How will you remind yourself of your goal and of your plan?**

There was a 2017 study from Dominican University that found if you just write down your goals, you are 42% more likely to achieve them. Another study showed that if you look at your goals every day, you're way more likely to achieve them.

If you remind yourself of what you are trying to do, the brain has a tendency to forget. We all set New Year's resolutions and goals, and then a week or two goes by and we've completely forgotten what the goal actually was.

**There is a lot of value in just reminding yourself to look at the goal.** How do you remind yourself?

- **Daily journaling practice**
- **Weekly review**
- **Stick blocks of time in the calendar:** For example, if one of my goals is the visceral fat thing and I want to run three times a week, it's super nice to put those three blocks of running in the calendar and set it to repeat for the next 3-6 months. Every time I look at the calendar, I'm reminding myself that this was a thing important to me
- **Use an app:** My team and I built an app called Momentum, which is a habit tracker with accountability squads built in. It gives you notifications every day—I get a notification to remind me to do my 10 minutes of stretching every day
- **Vision board as desktop wallpaper:** I have a vision board that I've created as my desktop wallpaper. This reminds me of some of my goals every single time I go on my computer

**The question:** What are the reminders I can implement into my life that will actually help me stick to the plan?

### Component #3: Accountability

Most of us really struggle to hold ourselves accountable to ourselves for the goals and plans we would like to achieve. **Having some kind of system where you get an accountability squad or an accountability buddy to help hold you accountable is enormously helpful.**

My team and I have an online mentorship program called the Lifestyle Business Academy where we mentor people one-on-one to help them build their lifestyle businesses. A key component of that is accountability:
- We check in on you every single week to make sure you're doing the things you said you were going to do
- We give you a Google Sheet scorecard to fill in every single week: How many hours you worked, how much content you posted, how many discovery calls you took
- We have a checklist process to help you go through figuring out your niche and your offer
- If we see that you're falling behind, we ping you a message: "Hey Johnny, noticed that you're falling a little behind on X. What's going on? Anything we can help with?"

It's an accountability system more than anything else.

**If you just spend 5 minutes defining:**
1. Your tracking system
2. Your reminders
3. Some sort of accountability system (whether a friend, family member, WhatsApp group, or an app with accountability squads)

...all of these things in the system component are again going to make you way more likely to actually follow your plan and achieve your goal.
